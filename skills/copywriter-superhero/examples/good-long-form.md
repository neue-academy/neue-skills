A cold-traffic sales letter for a £95/month bookkeeping service aimed at independent
building contractors. Deliberately not a Neue Academy brief: this file exists to prove the
skill works on any offer, with no brand codex supplied.

### Triage
- **Regime:** long-form persuasive
- **Deliverable:** sales letter, ten stages, roughly 1,300 words
- **Awareness:** problem-aware — they feel the cash gap, and blame themselves for it
- **Traffic:** cold, from a trade-publication ad
- **Framework:** PASTOR — story and testimony carry a sceptical trade audience
- **Length call:** long-form required. £1,140 a year, cold traffic, unfamiliar mechanism

## Headline

You did the work in March. You get paid in June. Ledgerline closes that gap to 12 days.

## Hook

A roofer in Leeds sent me his books last October. 214 invoices, 41 of them unpaid, £68,000
sitting in other people's accounts. He had been trading for nine years. Not once, on any
Monday morning of those nine years, had he known what he was actually owed.

## Problem — every contractor is running a bank, and nobody told them

Here is the arithmetic nobody explains before you go self-employed.

You buy the materials. You pay the labour on Friday. Then you invoice, and you wait 47
days, because that is the average across UK construction. Meanwhile the merchant wants
settling in 30. So you cover the difference out of your own account, every month, for the
privilege of doing the work.

That is the first-order cost. The second-order cost is worse.

At £68,000 outstanding on a 47-day cycle, you are lending roughly £8,800 a month at 0%.
Do that for a year and you have financed somebody else's business to the tune of a small
van. Miss a VAT deadline in the middle of it and HMRC adds a 2% surcharge, then 4%. Three
missed quarters puts you on a payment plan, and a payment plan on your file changes what
your merchant will extend you.

Now the part that actually ends businesses. You stop bidding on the big jobs. Not because
you cannot do them — because you cannot float them for seven weeks. The ceiling on your
company stops being your skill and becomes your cash cycle. Most contractors never notice
the swap. They just assume they have found their level.

Another year of this costs you the two jobs you did not bid, the 6% you did not charge, and
roughly 90 evenings at the kitchen table with a shoebox of receipts.

## Credibility — I read 1,900 contractor ledgers before I built anything

I spent six years as a credit controller for a builders' merchant in Sheffield. My job was
chasing contractors for money they did not have yet. I saw the same pattern 1,900 times:
good trades, full order books, no idea which invoice was late until the bank told them.

I am not an accountant. I am the person who used to phone you on day 45.

## Mechanism — the retention ledger, not the shoebox

Standard bookkeeping records what happened. That is why it arrives too late to be any use:
your accountant tells you in October what you should have known in March.

Ledgerline runs one ledger backwards from cash. Every invoice enters with three dates
attached — issued, contractually due, and historically likely, based on what that specific
client has actually done across your last twelve invoices to them. Not their terms. Their
behaviour.

That third date is the whole mechanism.

Once every invoice carries a likely-payment date, three things stop depending on your
memory. You see your true cash position for any Monday in the next 90 days. You see
which client is quietly financing themselves with your money, ranked. And chasing starts on
day 31 by default, without you deciding to be the difficult one, because the ledger sends
it, not you.

Your accountant cannot do this. They work from closed periods, quarterly, in arrears. The
software you already own cannot do it either: Xero and QuickBooks both record the due date
your client agreed to, then treat silence as compliance until you go looking.
