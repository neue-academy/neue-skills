# Good editorial — passing example

Two forms in one file, because the editorial regime covers both: a non-fiction chapter
opening, and a screenplay scene. No CTA, no offer, no persuasion. The reader continues or
stops, and that decision is made line by line.

```bash
python3 skills/copywriter-superhero/scripts/score_copy.py \
  skills/copywriter-superhero/examples/good-editorial.md --regime editorial
```

Adverbs and repeated openers are BLOCK findings here. Rhythm variance is the tightest gate
in any regime, because uniform sentence length is both the loudest machine tell and the
fastest route to a bored reader.

## Part one — non-fiction chapter opening

### Chapter 4: The Cost of Being Right

In February 1985, a Boeing 747 flying from Taipei to Los Angeles rolled onto its back at
41,000 feet and fell for two and a half minutes. It dropped 30,000 feet. The captain had
19,000 flight hours. He was, by every measure his airline used, one of the most capable
pilots in Asia.

The engine had failed. That part was routine, and he had trained for it perhaps a hundred
times.

What he had not trained for was disagreement.

His flight engineer saw the airspeed problem eleven seconds before the roll and said so.
The captain, holding a fix he had already decided on, did not hear it as information. He
heard it as a challenge to a judgement he had spent nineteen years earning. In the cockpit
voice recorder there is a gap of four seconds after the engineer speaks. Nobody says
anything. Then the aircraft goes over.

The plane landed at San Francisco with its landing gear doors torn away and two crew
injured. Everyone lived, which is why this story is a lesson and not a memorial.

I have watched the same four seconds happen in a boardroom in Leeds, in an operating theatre
in Bristol, and across a kitchen table in my own house. Somebody with standing decides.
Somebody without standing sees the problem. Between them sits a silence that costs
everything, and the silence is never about the facts. It is about what it would mean to be
the person who was wrong.

This chapter is about that silence — where it comes from, why expertise makes it worse
rather than better, and what the airline industry did about it that the rest of us have not.

The uncomfortable part comes at the end. The fix works, it has been measured across four
decades of accident data, and almost nobody outside aviation will adopt it, because it costs
the senior person something they are unwilling to spend.

## Part two — screenplay scene

### INT. GRANGE ROAD SURGERY, WAITING ROOM — 7:40 AM

Six plastic chairs. One occupied. MARGARET FYFE, 71, coat buttoned to the throat, holds a
paper number: 04.

A reception hatch slides open. DEEPA, 30s, in yesterday's cardigan.

**DEEPA**
Mrs Fyfe? He's running behind.

**MARGARET**
I know. I'm early.

Deepa hesitates, then slides the hatch shut. Margaret takes out a folded envelope. Opens
it. A hospital letter, creased soft at the folds from being read.

She reads it again anyway.

The hatch opens.

**DEEPA**
Twenty minutes, he says.

**MARGARET**
That's fine.

**DEEPA**
There's tea in the—

**MARGARET**
I know where the tea is, love.

Deepa withdraws. Margaret folds the letter along its old creases, puts it back in the
envelope, and puts the envelope in her handbag. She closes the clasp.

Then she takes it out again.

### INT. GRANGE ROAD SURGERY, CONSULTING ROOM — 8:05 AM

DR AMIN, 40s, reads a screen. Margaret sits with her handbag on her knees, clasp shut.

**DR AMIN**
So. The hospital's written to you.

**MARGARET**
They have.

**DR AMIN**
And you've read it.

A beat. Margaret looks at the handbag.

**MARGARET**
My husband did the reading.

Dr Amin stops looking at the screen.

## Why this passes

**Both parts open on a question, not a summary.** The 747 falls in the first sentence, and
the reader wants to know how a 19,000-hour pilot ends up inverted. Margaret is early for an
appointment she is dreading, and the letter she keeps taking out is the question. Neither
opening explains what the piece is about.

**The chapter follows the shape.** Concrete opening, then the turn ("What he had not trained
for was disagreement"), then the build, then the complication that stops it reading as a
sales pitch: the fix exists and almost nobody adopts it.

**The scene has a goal, an obstacle, and a turn.** Margaret wants to be seen. The obstacle
is delay, and then her own reluctance. The turn is six words — "My husband did the reading"
— and it changes everything the reader assumed. Enter late, leave early: the scene ends on
Dr Amin looking up, not on his reply.

**Subtext carries the dialogue.** "I know where the tea is, love" is about neither tea nor
knowledge. Nobody states a feeling. No adverb tells the reader how a line was said.

**Rhythm is deliberate.** A 39-word sentence about the boardroom in Leeds is followed by
"Somebody with standing decides." Four words. Then three. The cv gate exists to force
exactly this habit, and it is the difference between prose that moves and prose that idles.

**Specificity does the persuading.** 41,000 feet, eleven seconds, four seconds, six plastic
chairs, number 04, yesterday's cardigan. Two concrete details beat five abstract ones, in
both forms.
